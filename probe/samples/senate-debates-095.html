                    Pregnancy and Infant Loss Awareness Month

                    Hon. Mohamed-Iqbal Ravalia: Honourable senators, October is Pregnancy and Infant Loss Awareness Month, and October 15 marks Pregnancy and Infant Loss Remembrance Day — a time to honour the lives that were hoped for, the lives that were lost and the love that remains.

                    For countless women and families, pregnancy begins with the quiet dream of a future yet to unfold. When a pregnancy ends in loss or an infant’s life is cut tragically short, those dreams are interrupted, leaving behind a sorrow that words can seldom capture.

                    Whether through miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, stillbirth or infant loss, each experience is deeply personal. Yet, too often, the pain is carried in silence, unseen or unacknowledged.

                    Throughout my 35 years practising family medicine, I had the privilege of supporting patients through both the hope and anticipation of pregnancy and the profound heartbreak of loss. Despite how common these experiences are, they too often remain surrounded by silence and stigma.

                    Across Canada, women and families continue to navigate these experiences without adequate support. Beyond physical recovery, many face emotional distress, isolation and the difficult task of returning to everyday life while coping with an often invisible loss.

                    Access to medical follow-up, mental health services and, importantly, bereavement counselling remains uneven, particularly in rural and remote communities.

                    We must move beyond awareness toward meaningful action. This means strengthening national data collection, advancing research into the physical and psychological impacts of pregnancy and infant loss and working across jurisdictions to ensure families have access to timely, compassionate and continuous care.

                    Honourable senators, grief does not allow for a timeline, nor does love end when a life is lost. Even the briefest of lives can leave an enduring imprint on the hearts of those who loved them.

                    To every woman and family who has experienced pregnancy or infant loss: Your experience matters. May you find comfort in knowing that you are seen, your loss is acknowledged and you do not have to carry it alone.

                    Thank you. Meegwetch.

                    World Ostomy Day

                    Hon. Wanda Thomas Bernard: Honourable senators, World Ostomy Day was October 3. For this World Ostomy Day statement, I want to begin with two simple words: Thank you. I want to thank the partners, families and friends who stand alongside people living with an ostomy.

                    I know from my own experience as a spouse what that support can mean. Acceptance, encouragement and unconditional love can make an enormous difference in helping someone move beyond stigma and isolation and embrace a full life living with an ostomy.

                    Today, I especially want to highlight Ostomy Canada Society’s dedicated Spouses and Significant Others, or SASO, resource. SASO focuses on partners sharing responsibilities, emotional resilience, communication, support networks and advocacy, as well as celebrating milestones together. Thank you to the founders and members of SASO across Canada.

                    For the third year, I’m using World Ostomy Day to speak out and speak up for Canadians living with an ostomy. The messages I have received over the past three years remind me why speaking openly matters. People have thanked me for helping to break the silence and dispel the myths that still surround ostomies.

                    There is more work to do, including addressing what one person described as:

                    . . . the material gap in the emotional and financial support of those affected by this medical necessity in their day-to-day lives.

                    For me, this advocacy is also deeply personal. My late husband, George Bernard, lived a full and vibrant life with an ostomy for almost 20 years, supported by people who loved and accepted him. I continue to advocate for greater understanding and support for people living with an ostomy in memory of his ability to live life fully with joy and gratitude.

                    So, today, I thank every spouse and significant other who walks this journey alongside someone they love, and I stand with every person living with an ostomy.

                    Together, let us Step Up for Ostomy, break the silence, reduce stigma and ensure that no one has to walk this journey alone.

                    Hon. Pamela Wallin, O.C.

                    Hon. Flordeliz (Gigi) Osler: Honourable senators, it’s my privilege, on behalf of the Canadian Senators Group, to pay tribute to our newly retired colleague the Honourable Pamela Wallin. She leaves behind a chamber made sharper and more focused by her presence. Before the Senate, Pamela spent decades telling Canadians about the world.

                    Born in Moose Jaw, Saskatchewan, she brought her trademark Prairie pragmatism to national journalism. Her career moved quickly through radio, print and television news media. In 1982, she was the only female Canadian TV journalist to report from the Falkland Islands War. In 1985, she became the first woman in Canada to head a national television bureau. In 1992, she became the first woman in Canada to co-anchor a nightly news show.

                    Pamela Wallin shattered those glass ceilings, and for years we welcomed her into our living rooms, watching her cut through political noise to relentlessly pursue the truth. Her conversational yet laser-focused interviewing style invariably led to her leaning in and saying to the person she was interviewing, “Since you asked . . .” That phrase ultimately became the title of her bestselling 1998 autobiography.

                    When called on to serve as Canada’s consul general in New York after 9/11, she accepted without hesitation. She brought poise and clarity to the international stage, serving as a steady anchor for Canada-U.S. relations at a time when the world needed stability.

                    (1340)

                    In the Senate, Pamela was a powerhouse. Her legislative legacy includes her fierce, deeply personal advocacy for medical assistance in dying reform. Driven by memories of her own parents, she fought passionately for advance directives, wanting Canadians facing degenerative illnesses to retain their dignity and the right to choose their own path.

                    On October 6, 2026, she stood in this chamber to deliver her final speech. Her speech was on advance directives, and she did not mince words. She demanded an end to what she called the “perverse diagnosis lottery,” where a person facing dementia is denied the right to choose their final path before losing the capacity to consent. Quoting Dylan Thomas, she urged us to not go gentle into this good fight for dignity in our final days.

                    Dearest Pamela, we in the Canadian Senators Group will miss your wit, your firm convictions and your unshakeable spirit. Though your seat may be empty, we know your voice will never be quiet. As you close this remarkable chapter, allow me to close in the words of a true broadcaster: “That’s all for today. Thank you for having been with us, and bye for now.”

                    The Late Simoget Nikate’en Frances Sampson

                    Hon. Mary Jane McCallum: Honourable senators, it is with deep sadness that we acknowledge the passing of Hereditary Chief Simoget Nikate’en Frances Sampson on October 12, 2026. The Gitxsan Nation has lost a respected leader, whose life was dedicated to her faith, her people, her hereditary responsibilities and the protection of her laxyip, or territory.

                    As a child, she was hospitalized at Miller Bay Hospital, where Nikate’en experienced an act of kindness from a nurse, which left a lasting impression on her. That experience helped inspire her to pursue a career in nursing services, where she could extend the same compassion and care to others.

                    Her unwavering faith in God guided her throughout her life and led her to serve her community as a Corps Sergeant-Major with the Salvation Army. Through her faith and service, she demonstrated a steadfast commitment to helping others and supporting those in need.

                    As a hereditary leader of the Gitxsan Nation, Simoget Nikate’en fulfilled her responsibilities to the best of her ability, having been groomed for this role from birth. Guided by Gitxsan ayookw, or law, and the responsibilities entrusted to her as Hereditary Chief, she helped guide her wilp, or house, in its decisions, actions and cultural practices. Her leadership was rooted in the teachings, laws, traditions and obligations passed down through generations.

                    Simoget Nikate’en also played an instrumental role as a translator during the historic Delgamuukw court case. Through this important contribution, she helped ensure that Gitxsan voices, Traditional Knowledge and Oral Traditions could be communicated within a legal process that would have lasting significance for Indigenous rights and title in Canada.

                    Simoget Nikate’en Frances Sampson leaves behind a legacy of faith, courage, responsibility and unwavering commitment to her people and her laxyip. Her contributions will be remembered with respect and gratitude by her wilp, the Gitxsan Nation and all those whose lives she touched. May her legacy continue to guide those lives she has touched.

                    The Late Stephen Jarislowsky, C.C., O.C.

                    Hon. Tony Loffreda: Honourable senators, I rise today to pay tribute to a Canadian icon, Stephen Jarislowsky, a successful businessman and remarkable philanthropist, who passed away on October 8 at the age of 101.

                    Mr. Jarislowsky was known for his outstanding achievements as an entrepreneur and investor, as well as his many contributions to our economic prosperity. However, he may be best remembered and admired for his philanthropy and desire to make our country better.

                    I often say, “Integrity is non-negotiable.” It was a principle Mr. Jarislowsky shared. He believed deeply in ethics, once observing:

                    Ethics are a good way of getting through life. And if you practise ethics, people will respect you, people will honour you, people will believe you and people will follow you.

                    I had the privilege of meeting him on many occasions. Our conversations often focused on the economy. He was never one to hold back his views. I fondly recall a discussion at an event celebrating Mark Carney’s appointment as Governor of the Bank of England, when we reflected on the 2008 financial crisis, the resilience of Canada’s economy and the stability of our banks and real estate markets. His insights reflected sound judgment, rigorous analysis and an impressive understanding of economic policy and global trends.

                    Colleagues, I would also like to share my memories of our last meeting on September 1 at his home, just days before his one hundred and first birthday. Senator Gignac also had the honour of joining us that day.

                    For the past year, I have advocated for a national longevity economy strategy because I believe Canada must better prepare its economy, its financial and health care systems, as well as our labour market for longer, healthier lives.

                    Given the work, focus and reputation of the Jarislowsky Foundation, I welcomed the opportunity to discuss this vision with him and his team. He was fully engaged in our discussion, and he obviously understood the need for a national strategy.

                    As expected, our conversation also turned to Canada’s economic future. He shared thoughtful perspectives on improving productivity and addressing the affordability crisis. Senator Gignac and I both feel fortunate to have had this unique opportunity with him just weeks before his passing.

                    Honourable senators, Canada owes a great deal to Stephen Jarislowsky. His generosity, integrity and commitment to our country leave a lasting legacy.

                    On behalf of Canadians, I thank him for his extraordinary contributions and for the example he set as a great Canadian and a kind human being.

                    Islamic Heritage Month

                    Hon. Salma Ataullahjan: Honourable senators, a donation can tell us something about the person who gives it. It can also tell us something about the tradition that taught them to give.

                    During Islamic Heritage Month, I want to reflect on a principle at the heart of Islam: that a portion of one’s wealth carries an obligation to others. Zakat, one of the five pillars of Islam, gives that principle practical force. For Muslims who meet its conditions, giving is a religious duty. It asks a person to consider what they owe to someone whose circumstances may be very different from their own.

                    There is a powerful understanding of society in that obligation. Our comfort at home does not lessen our responsibility to a neighbour in need. Those who cannot give financially can offer their time and effort in service, a gift of themselves whose worth cannot be measured in money.

                    That understanding deserves a place in how we speak about Islamic heritage. Heritage lives in the habits people teach their children. A child watching a parent set money aside for someone in need is learning what prosperity is for. A young volunteer helping prepare a meal is discovering that service requires effort and that another person’s dignity deserves care.

                    In Canada, Muslim charitable organizations and local congregations put these teachings to work. The public may see the assistance without knowing the conviction behind it. Islamic Heritage Month gives us an opportunity to appreciate both. It also invites us to look more closely at a faith we too often encounter through the urgency of the news. There is much to discover in the ordinary, deliberate practice of doing good and in the generations of people who have sustained it.

                    (1350)

                    Honourable senators, a country’s character takes shape through what its people feel responsible for. To this shared work, Muslim Canadians bring a tradition that makes generosity an obligation and asks those with enough to remember those without.

                    This October, I would like to honour that inheritance and the Muslim Canadians whose generosity and service give it life. Thank you.

                    Hon. Senators: Hear, hear.

                    Finance

                    Cost of Living

                    Hon. Salma Ataullahjan: Government leader, yesterday’s HungerCount report by Food Banks Canada revealed yet another record-breaking year for food bank use under your government’s watch.

                    The statistics are alarming: More than 2.3 million food bank visits were recorded, up 9% from last year and nearly 117% since 2019, and children now account for one third of all visits.

                    Senator Moreau, these numbers represent real Canadians whose incomes are being overwhelmed by the rising cost of housing, fuel and everyday essentials. Why are your government’s policies failing to ease these pressures and reduce reliance on food banks across Canada?

                    Hon. Pierre Moreau (Government Representative in the Senate): Thank you, Senator Ataullahjan, for your question. It’s a very important one. That’s precisely the reason the government is acting directly and precisely to quickly support Canadians with respect to rising food prices through the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit with the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit Act. Also, with the National School Food Program, a family of four will receive $1,800 this year and $1,400 for the next four years. The second payment for that program was made on October 6.

                    The government is also building a more independent and affordable food system for all with the launch of the National Food Security Strategy, which will be backed by $3 billion in investment, including $1 billion to expand food terminals and $130 million for the Competition Bureau and the Competition Tribunal to investigate, prevent and combat anti-competitive business practices, so we’re acting to ensure that food is available and affordable for all Canadians.

                    Senator Ataullahjan: Senator Moreau, a troubling consequence of this crisis is that as Canadians’ disposable income shrinks, their ability to support food banks is shrinking as well. If your government cannot reverse the conditions driving the demand for food banks, what specific measures will it take to prevent declining donations from worsening the crisis?

                    Senator Moreau: The facts seem to contradict your premise. For a second consecutive month, grocery prices grew slower than overall inflation. The government is acting on affordability when it comes to groceries, vulnerable Canadians, children and the housing market to provide Canadians what they need. The fact is that the results are there.

                    Global Affairs

                    Lawful Access Bill, 2026

                    Second Reading

                    On the Order:

                    Resuming debate on the motion of the Honourable Senator Dhillon, seconded by the Honourable Senator Varone, for the second reading of Bill C-22, An Act respecting lawful access.

                    Hon. Denise Batters: Honourable senators, I rise today as opposition critic to speak to the second reading of Bill C-22, An Act respecting lawful access. The bill seeks to modernize the framework of giving access to information and data in the context of criminal and national security investigations.

                    It consists of two parts. Part 1 addresses the investigative powers relating to subscriber information, computer data, location and transmission data, and exigent circumstances.

                    Part 2 enacts a new regime applying to electronic service providers, or ESPs, called the “Supporting Authorized Access to Information Act.” This allows the cabinet to designate what are termed “core providers” and impose technical and operational obligations on them. These obligations are related to the extraction of and access to information, the installation and maintenance of equipment and the retention of certain metadata for a maximum period of time, originally a year, but now changed to six months through an amendment at a House of Commons committee.

                    An administrative monetary penalties regime will be applied for non-compliance: up to $50,000 for an individual and $250,000 for an entity, and up to $100,000 for an individual and $500,000 for an entity for a criminal conviction.

                    Many of the major details of the bill will be left to regulation, which is a significant cause for concern.

                    In this modern age of rapidly evolving technology, global interconnectivity and information explosion, it is crucial that law enforcement and security agencies have quick access to the data they require in an investigation or an urgent situation.

                    The counterbalance, of course, is the constitutional protection of the Charter and privacy rights of Canadians. Bill C-22 is at the intersection of these issues, and it is very delicate to determine the right balance for Parliament to legislate.

                    This is the seventh time governments have tried to propose a lawful access bill — three times under a Conservative government and now an additional four times under a Liberal government. The attempt immediately before this was with Bill C-2, the Carney Liberal government’s first bill.

                    (1430)

                    Bill C-2 required warrantless disclosure of information from any service provider in Canada in the absence of court oversight. Thankfully, the government was forced to back down from that iteration of the legislation, but Bill C-22 repackages a lot of what was in that bill.

                    Even with all the attempts at lawful access legislation, the Liberal government has still failed to strike the proper balance between ensuring security and protecting Canadians’ privacy and Charter rights. It’s not just me who feels that way. The official opposition in the House of Commons, legal experts and academics, civil liberties organizations, technology companies and cybersecurity experts all continue to express concerns even though the bill was amended in the House of Commons. There is little doubt that Canada’s absence of proper lawful access legislation makes Canada vulnerable. Even though the Liberal government has been in power for 11 years to this point, they still haven’t accomplished this.

                    In its briefing material on the bill, the government notes: “Canada risks an increasing reputation of being a haven for criminal and threat actor communication activities.”

The Carney government — and the Trudeau government that predated it — dismantled the regime of tough criminal legislation to create weak Liberal criminal laws and a culture of lax enforcement.

                    Further, the Liberal government has taken minimal and very late action on foreign interference, leaving Canadians vulnerable to predation by unsavoury transnational actors. This Liberal government has a very bad habit of only taking law enforcement’s advice when it really wants to.

                    I certainly appreciate the need for Canada’s national security and law enforcement organizations to have timely access to the information they need to keep Canadians safe, but we must tread carefully where the rights of Canadians are concerned. Further, Parliament must also be cautious not to inadvertently cause additional security vulnerabilities that could be exploited if they fell into the wrong hands.

                    As my Conservative MP colleague Chak Au stated during the House of Commons debate on this bill:

                    The question is not whether we need lawful access; the question is how we implement it safely and without introducing new risks.

There was the controversial Bill C-11, the Online Streaming Act.                     The Town of Lincoln is only some 50 years old as a town, but it’s amongst the oldest settlements certainly in Upper Canada, which includes the little hamlets of Vineland, where I grew up, and Jordan which is famous for its revived tourism sector, which I’ll get to. It has the Town of Beamsville. It’s got the little hamlet of Campden. It came together as a result of the amalgamation some 52 years ago into the Town of Lincoln. Why Lincoln? Because it’s actually the residual name of the County of Lincoln, which was the historic county on the northern half of the Niagara Peninsula.

                    The Town of Lincoln has also been rebranded in some fashion as a tourist destination for what they call the Niagara Benchlands, with benchlands being the distinct stepped architecture of the landscape. As you drive around the peninsula, you have the escarpment on your south side and a series of ledges, which host increasingly tender fruits and wine. The benchlands have the largest concentration of craft beverage producers in Canada, which includes breweries, cideries, distilleries and, of course, wineries.

                    The Town of Lincoln, with some 23,000 people spread over this territory between Grimsby and St. Catharines, has over 50 individual wine labels, most of them family-related businesses. Their wines are both regionally and internationally known. There are award-winning wines, like Hidden Bench Estate Winery, Westcott Vineyards and Vineland Estates Winery — three of my favourite wines. I would invite you to go to the restaurant at Vineland Estates up on the bench. On a clear day, sip a glass of wine and look across the lake to Toronto and say, “Aren’t you glad you’re not in that traffic?”

                    These wines are not the same as those in the Niagara-on-the-Lake area, which are further east. Wines from this microclimate are influenced by ancient limestone and glacial drift, giving them their signature minerality and complexity, which are perfect for Rieslings, Cabernet Francs, Chardonnays and icewines.

                    After a long day of sampling wines from across the region, a dinner at Restaurant Pearl Morissette may be in order — assuming you have a reservation. This exquisite fine dining experience is internationally recognized with two Michelin Stars, one of only two restaurants in all of Canada with this distinction. Moreover, it also has a Michelin Green Star, a relatively new designation for restaurants recognized with a Michelin Star and that are also at the forefront of sustainability and eco-friendly gastronomy. Restaurant Pearl Morissette is the only restaurant in the country holding both two Michelin Stars and a Michelin Green Star.

                    According to the Michelin Guide, a two-star designation means excellent cooking that is “worth a detour.” So take that detour off the Queen Elizabeth Way.

                    Tourism and hospitality support more than 1,700 jobs in the Niagara Benchlands with employment driven by these craft beverage producers and restaurants but also in the sectors of accommodation, agri-tourism, cultural and visitor and outdoor experiences, with the Bruce Trail being one.

                    Tourism is a key driver of rural prosperity in the Town of Lincoln, with visitor spending supporting local businesses, farms, wineries and local producers. Tourism helps to preserve local agricultural heritage and protect the rural landscapes that define my community.

                    As I alluded, in order to understand the nation building of tourism, we must also understand a bit of our history and where we come from. Through the Lincoln Museum & Cultural Centre in the village of Jordan, the stories of Lincoln’s cultural, social and agricultural heritage are preserved through exhibitions, educational programs and world-class archives in its basement. The Lincoln Museum & Cultural Centre acts as a heritage hub for the Town of Lincoln and offers intimate tours of the Fry House and the Jordan Schoolhouse.

                    The Fry House is a two-storey log dwelling built around 1815 by two German-speaking Mennonite settlers from Pennsylvania. It was a family home until 1892. In later years, the house was used as a chicken coop and a playhouse. Originally situated in Vineland, the house was carefully moved in 1959 to its current location on the Lincoln Museum & Cultural Centre property. The Fry House is a rare surviving example of the Pennsylvania Dutch traditional architecture in Canada.

                    The Jordan Schoolhouse is a one-room school that served the children of Jordan for almost 90 years. Inside, students would learn moral instruction, hands-on skills and, of course, the three Rs. As many as 60 children, ranging from ages 6 to 16, would be taught by a single teacher in one room, putting even Ontario class sizes of today to shame. The school was closed in 1948 and subsequently purchased and returned to the community, where it was opened as Jordan’s first museum. The schoolhouse was later restored to reflect a classroom circa 1908.

                    Beyond Jordan, the Town of Lincoln is home to a collection of historic villages and hamlets, to which I already referred. I know that Senator Black visited the area last weekend. It was all the news in the town. He participated there in the unveiling of a statue to honour the Farmerettes, which reflects its agricultural roots. The Beamsville high school is where I went to high school, and the community remains rich in culture and heritage, having benefited from major waves of immigration over the years.

                    I’d also like to point out that Lincoln celebrates and honours the history of the Attawandaron — or the Neutral Nation — the first known inhabitants of the land. Developed in collaboration with Indigenous partners, Sho’aríshon Park — which was recently opened — features permanent and evolving public art, a rain garden with native species, interpretive elements and gathering spaces that foster learning, reflection and connection to the area’s Indigenous heritage. The community is keen on celebrating its Indigenous history. Oral histories and archaeological evidence suggest Indigenous presence in the Niagara Region for at least 13,000 years. The name of one Neutral group, the Onguiaahra, is believed to be part of the origin of the word “Niagara.”

                    Through its museums, parks, heritage properties and cultural programming, the wonderful Town of Lincoln celebrates the stories of all its peoples. Agriculture and communities have defined the Niagara Benchlands for generations. Nation building isn’t just a modern philosophy; it’s what humans have always done. In Niagara and in the Town of Lincoln, our nation-building past has helped create a stepping stone for nation building now, which will help future generations create more steps into the future.

                    The value of tourism should not be understated for all the Niagara Region. Tourism is where Canadians learn about their home, their history and how to preserve their country in all of its glories and regional dimensions.

                    I encourage Canadians to learn more about all the regions of Canada. Surely, when you’re thinking of going to Niagara Falls next, get off the road at Christie Street, Ontario Street or, best yet, Vineland and Victoria Avenue. Thank you, colleagues.
